Abstract

Global warming is changing the conditions in which both humans and fungi live and evolve. These changes may be long-term, in the case of increasing temperature, humidity and changes in atmospheric composition, or acute due to geo-meteological disturbances such as tornadoes, floods, wildfires, dust storms and earthquakes. Long-term changes mainly favour selection of fungi suited to the new conditions, while acute events can disperse fungi beyong typical ecological niches and cause infection in compromised or injured hosts. The combined stresses associated with these events may also trigger fungal responses that increase their ability to cause disease.

This chapter reviews experimental evidence for how fungi respond to environmental pressures and distinguishes established findings from proposed explanations. Three findings are central: growth at higher temperatures can increase virulence and tolerance to oxidative stress in Mucorales through calcineurin signalling; host temperatures can trigger genetic changes, including the movement of transposable elements in Cryptococcus and the emergence of pan-resistant, highly virulent mutants in Rhodosporidiobolus; and physical stresses such as extreme shear can temporarily increase virulence through some of the same cellular pathways involved in heat adaptation.

These findings do not establish that climate change will cause a human fungal pandemic. Mammalian body temperatures still prevent many fungi from growing, and heat tolerance alone is a poor predictor of whether a fungus can cause disease. Recent examples of emerging fungal threats such as Candida auris, azole-resistant Aspergillus fumigatus and terbinafine-resistant Trichophyton indotineae reflect pressures from agricultural and clinical antifungal use, healthcare practices and poverty.

We propose a framework for assessing the strength of the evidence, testable criteria for identifying climate’s role, and research priorities focused on improving study design for evaluating phenotypic and genotypic adaption of fungi to climate change

1 Introduction: Fungi in a changing environment

1.1 The fungal kingdom and environment

Fungi are a diverse group with a long evolutionary history, and most live in soil, water, decaying matter or in association with plants. Conservative estimates place global fungal diversity between 2.2 and 3.8 million species, of which fewer than 150,000 have been formally described (Hawksworth and Lücking 2017). Of that vast pool, only a few hundred species have been implicated in human disease, and yet the clinical burden of fungal disease is substantial. Current global estimates attribute roughly 6.5 million invasive fungal infections and 3.8 million deaths annually to fungal disease, a figure that has risen sharply as estimation methods have improved (Denning 2024).

Fungi play a major role in moving carbon and nutrients through land ecosystems. Mycorrhizal fungi, which live in association with plant roots, are estimated to receive carbon equivalent to about 13 billion tonnes of CO₂ from plants each year (Hawkins et al. 2023). Researchers have therefore argued that fungal responses to environmental change matter for the planet as a whole (Case et al. 2025). Most fungi support ecosystem functions, but some can cause opportunistic infections. This chapter asks whether human-driven environmental change could increase the number of fungi capable of causing disease or make existing fungal pathogens more harmful.

Only recently have researchers gained the tools to study this question directly. For much of the twentieth century, studies of fungal adaptation focused on resistance to agricultural fungicides or laboratory evolution in the yeast Saccharomyces. Three developments have broadened this work: affordable whole-genome sequencing of fungi collected from the environment, infection models using invertebrates and tissues, and detailed climate data that can be linked to where and when fungi were collected. Together, these tools allow researchers to investigate how fungi adapt in natural settings and how those changes affect their ability to cause disease (Nnadi and Carter 2021; Seidel et al. 2024).

1.2 Two axes of change: Chronic warming and acute disturbance

Distinguishing between two types of climate pressure is essential to to analysis of climate effects (Fig. 1).

The chronic axis comprises slow, directional change: rising mean surface temperature, shifts in aridity and humidity regimes, elevated atmospheric CO₂, altered ultraviolet exposure, glacial retreat and permafrost thaw, and sea-level rise with coastal submersion. These act principally as selective pressures. They change which genotypes in an environmental population are favoured, over timescales of many fungal generations, and they change the geographic envelope within which a species can complete its life cycle.

The acute axis comprises discrete, high-energy events: tornadoes and tropical cyclones, extreme rainfall and flooding, wildfires, dust storms, earthquakes and tsunamis. These act principally through dispersal, through contact with injured hosts and displacement, and through the delivery of intense, combinatorial physical stress to fungal cells — shear, desiccation, heat, oxidative challenge — over minutes to days rather than decades. The recognition that this second axis constitutes a coherent field of study in its own right, distinct from classical environmental mycology, is the contribution of “disaster microbiology” (Smith and Casadevall 2022; Smith and Casadevall 2023).

Both axes are modified throughout by a socioeconomic gradient. Poverty, armed conflict, forced migration, urban crowding and unequal access to diagnostics and antifungals determine who is exposed, who is susceptible, and who is treated . Socioeconomic factors can play a larger role in fungal diseases than any single biological variable discussed in this chapter (Jenks et al. 2023).

1.3 Scope of this chapter

We restrict the chapter to fungal adaptation and its consequences. Sect. 2 sets out the thermal restriction hypothesis, the quantitative argument on which most climate–mycology reasoning rests, and its principal weakness. Sect. 3 and Sect. 4 separate phenotypic (plastic, reversible) from genotypic (heritable) adaptation. Sect. 5 tests the framework against six sentinel emergences, grading the strength of climate attribution in each. Sect. 6 treats anthropogenic co-drivers that are often mislabelled as climate effects. Sect. 7 develops the disaster axis. Sect. 8 places human mycoses in the broader One Health context of plant, crop and wildlife disease, where climate-driven fungal mortality is already demonstrable. Sect. 9 addresses the pandemic question with explicit criteria, and Sect. 10 proposes a research and response agenda. Clinical management and the antifungal development pipeline are outside our scope; both are reviewed elsewhere (Lax et al. 2024).

2 The Thermal Restriction Hypothesis

2.1 Endothermy as a barrier to fungal infection

A leading explanation for why relatively few fungi infect mammals is that mammalian bodies are too warm for most fungi to grow. Evidence comes from a survey of fungi held in culture collections: as temperature increased, fewer species could grow. Above roughly 30 °C, each additional degree prevented a substantial further share of species from growing (Robert and Casadevall 2009). Models balancing the energy needed to maintain a warm body against protection from fungal infection found an optimum close to actual mammalian body temperatures. This suggests that protection from fungi may have helped drive the evolution of warm-bloodedness (Bergman and Casadevall 2010). A related hypothesis proposes that this protection helped mammals diversify after the Cretaceous–Paleogene mass extinction, when fungi are thought to have been abundant. This idea has prompted further research, but remains unproven (Casadevall 2012).

This framework makes a clear, testable prediction: sustained environmental warming should favour fungi that tolerate higher temperatures. Over time, more fungal species could become able to grow at mammalian body temperatures, increasing the number that could potentially cause infections, particularly in vulnerable hosts (Garcia-Solache and Casadevall 2010; Casadevall 2020).

2.2 The narrowing thermal exclusion zone

Two trends are narrowing this temperature gap (Fig. 2). The environment is warming, while human core temperature has fallen. A study of three groups covering more than 150 years of US records found that average body temperature fell steadily by about 0.03 °C per decade of birth. The modern average is now close to 36.6 °C, below the traditional value of 37.0 °C (Protsiv et al. 2020). The gap is therefore shrinking from both sides.

The size of the temperature gap matters. Most soil fungi grow best at temperatures roughly 10–20 °C below mammalian core temperature, making it unlikely that this thermal barrier will be bridged anytime soon for most fungi. Warming could, however, increase heat tolerance among fungi that already tolerate relatively high temperatures-i.e. fungi at the tail of the temperature spectrum closest to mammalian core temperatures. Because fungi capable of infecting mammals come from this group, even small changes could matter, despite little noticible change in the majority of “cooler” environmental fungi.

2.3 Thermal adaptation and pathogenicity

Growth at host temperature is necessary for pathogenicity, but it is only one of many requirements. A fungal lineage must also survive attack by the host’s innate immune system, extract nutrients from host tissue, tolerate host pH and hypoxia, and reach susceptible hosts through some route of exposure or transmission. Comparative genomic work on fungi, in which human pathogenicity has evolved repeatedly and independently, shows that it emerges from combinations of traits -i.e. thermotolerance, cell-wall remodelling, changes is secondary metabolism, nutrient scavenging and stress-response architecture. These combinations emerge along many different evolutionary trajectories rather than a single pathway(Rokas 2022). Therefore, environmental warming could enlarge over time the pool of candidate organisms from which opportunistic pathogens may be selected.

2.4 The specificity problem

The strongest objection to the thermal restriction argument is the lack of specificity as a barrier for pathogenicity. Tens of thousands of fungal species already grow at 37 °C without ever causing human disease. Thermatolerance is therefore an essential, but not exclusive identifier of pathogenicity. Therefore, it would be illogical to accept that growing thermotolerance alone in environmental fungal will lead to new human pathogens (Money 2024). The counterargument is that growth at mammalian body temperature tells us which fungi to focus on, not what will happen. It is a screening tool, not a measure of risk. Second, the useful climate question is not how many species can now grow at 37 °C, but whether heat stress selects more dangerous genotypes of environmental fungi that are drug-resistant, unusually virulent, or better able to evade the mammalian host immune system. The mechanistic basis of this question can be tested experimentally; and is the focus of Sect. 3 and Sect. 4,. Table 1 grades the evidence for each mechanism considered there. A second objection concerns attribution. Most published links between climate and fungal disease are correlations drawn from data collected for other purposes. Such data cannot separate climate from everything else that changed alongside it: better diagnostics, more surveillance, wider antifungal use, altered land use, a more susceptible population. We return to this in Sect. 4.6 and apply it to each case study in Sect. 5.

3 Phenotypic Adaptation: Plasticity Under Thermal and Physical Stress

Phenotypic adaptation to thermal or physical stress, means a change in the properties of a fungal cell or population that occurs without DNA sequence modification that reverses when the inducing stimulus is withdrawn. Fig. 3 summarizes these plastic responses alongside the heritable routes considered in Sect. 4.

3.1 How fungi acquire heat tolerance

3.1.1 The heat-shock machinery

The canonical response to increasing temperature is transcriptional reprogramming through heat-shock transcription factors, most prominently Hsf1, with downstream induction of chaperones and accumulation of the compatible solute trehalose (Morano et al. 2011). In C. albicans, Hsf1 activation is required not merely for thermal survival but for full virulence, establishing that thermal-stress machinery and pathogenicity are mechanistically coupled (Nicholls et al. 2010). The Hsp90 chaperone occupies a central node: it stabilizes the client proteins on which both stress signalling and drug-target function depend, and its pharmacological or genetic compromise sensitizes fungi to both heat and antifungals (Cowen et al. 2009). Hsp90 also constitutes the mechanistic bridge to Sect. 4, for reasons discussed in 4.5.

A related concept with direct climate relevance is priming. Sublethal stress exposure can leave a cell better able to survive a subsequent, more severe challenge. In fungi this phenomenon has been documented across thermal, oxidative and cell-wall stresses (Harish and Osherov 2022). Because climate-associated stresses are rarely delivered alone, priming is likely to be the rule rather than the exception in natural settings, and it complicates any inference from single-stress laboratory experiments to field behavior. Remarkably, fungal pathogens can retain a form of “adaptive memory”of prior host-associated stress that alters subsequent immune recognition (Brown et al. 2018).

3.1.2 Calcineurin signalling and morphogenesis at elevated temperature

The calcium–calmodulin-dependent protein phosphatase calcineurin is the best-characterized pathway linking thermal stress to fungal virulence. It is required for growth at host temperature, for morphogenesis and for cell-wall integrity across Cryptococcus, Aspergillus, Candida and the Mucorales. Inhibition of calcineurin by cyclosporine A or tacrolimus attenuates virulence of yeast and moulds in animal models (Steinbach et al. 2007; Juvvadi et al. 2016).

The calcineurin pathway provides the most direct experimental evidence that warming can increase virulence rather in a range of pathogenic fungi than merely facilitating growth rates. 3.2 Pigmentation, melanin and thermal buffering

Melanins are multifunctional fungal pigments that absorb radiation, quench free radicals, sequester metals and contribute to cell-wall rigidity. Their roles extend well beyond the virulence functions for which they are best known in Cryptococcus (Cordero and Casadevall 2017). Melanized fungi can even derive metabolic benefit from ionizing radiation, a finding that expanded the conception of what fungal pigments do (Dadachova et al. 2007). Because pigmentation modulates absorbed radiant energy, it also modulates cell temperature, which makes it a plausible target of selection under warming.

The most direct field evidence comes from a paired sampling design in an urban heat island. Fungi isolated from warmer Baltimore neighbourhoods showed greater thermotolerance and lighter pigmentation than those from cooler neighbourhoods, and the opportunistic pathogen Rhodotorula mucilaginosa from warmer areas resisted gradual heat challenge better (Smith et al. 2025). The lighter pigmentation is consistent with reduced radiant heat absorption being advantageous in a hotter microenvironment. However, it must be acknowledged that cool and warm neighbourhoods in a city differ in tree canopy, sun exposure, pedestrian and animal traffic, impervious surface and socioeconomic history, any of which could drive the observed differences. Treated as a natural experiment with acknowledged confounding, however, this is among the most useful datasets available, and the design deserves replication in other cities and climates.

3.3 Mechanical and hydrodynamic stress

Disaster conditions deliver physical stresses that are absent from conventional laboratory culture. Subjecting Mucorales to a tornadic shear challenge that recapitulates the hydrodynamic forces of a tornado was found to induce a hypervirulent phenotype in a fruit-fly infection model, an effect dependent on calcineurin and Hsp90 signalling and abolished by cyclosporine A or an Hsp90 inhibitor (Wurster et al. 2020). The phenotype is transient, reverting when the stress is removed, and is therefore plasticity rather than adaptation in the evolutionary sense. It is mediated by the same signalling nodes as thermal adaptation, which suggests a shared stress-integration architecture rather than stress-specific programs. And it provides a mechanistic hypothesis for an otherwise puzzling clinical observation: the clusters of aggressive, necrotizing cutaneous mucormycosis that follow tornadoes (Sect. 7.2).

3.4 Stress cross-talk

Taken together, the sections above describe a system in which thermal, oxidative, osmotic and mechanical stresses feed into a small number of shared signalling pathways (Hsf1/Hsp90, calcineurin, the HOG/SAPK ) that modulate morphogenesis, cell-wall remodelling, antifungal tolerance and virulence. A practical consequence is that in a warming, destabilized environment, fungi will rarely experience one stress at a time; they will experience heat during drought, shear and desiccation during storms, oxidative and thermal stress during fire. If cross-protection is the norm, single-stress dose–response experiments may systematically underestimate the phenotypic change that field organisms undergo. Multi-stress experimental designs are therefore not a refinement but a requirement discussed in Sect. 10.1.

4 Genotypic Adaptation: Mutagenesis, Genome Plasticity and Epigenetic Priming

If Sect. 3 concerned what a fungus does when it is warmed, this section concerns what warming does to its genome. The distinction is important, as only heritable change can accumulate across generations, respond to selection, and produce lineages that remain altered after conditions normalize.

4.1 Temperature-stimulated transposon mobilization

Their is strong evidence that growth at host temperature can increase mutations in Cryptococcus deneoformans. Transposable-element insertions were more frequent at 37 °C than at 30 °C and caused drug resistance in vitro. Such insertions were also detected during mouse infection (Gusa et al. 2020). A later genome-wide study found that the increased mutation burden at 37 °C was driven mainly by two mobile elements, Tcn12 and Cnl1. Tcn12 inserted primarily within genes, whereas Cnl1 accumulated near chromosome ends. The study also detected movements of all three elements examined in isolates recovered from infected mice, confirming that mobilization occurs within a host (Gusa et al. 2023). Together, these two studies provide a mechanistically complete account of a climate-relevant mutagenic process. Elevated temperature, whether environmental or host-derived, increased the rate at which a fungal population generates drug-resistant variants.

4.2 Host-temperature-induced mutagenesis and pan-antifungal resistance

An independent line of evidence comes from Rhodosporidiobolus fluvialis, a yeast from a genus containing species intrinsically resistant to azoles and echinocandins, which was recovered from two human infections. Exposure to mammalian body temperature induced mutagenesis and yielded mutants with markedly enhanced hyphal growth and hypervirulence in mice, and temperature-driven mutagenesis also generated pan-resistant derivatives in related species (Huang et al. 2024). However, the mechanisms underlying these mutations remain unclear, and it is not known whether similar responses occur in other basidiomycetous yeasts. Heat-exposure experiments combined with comparative genomic analyses across species would help establish how widespread this response is

4.3 Aneuploidy, copy-number variation and loss of heterozygosity

Large-scale genome rearrangement is the classical fungal route to rapid stress adaptation. Fluconazole resistance in C. albicans arises repeatedly through formation of a specific isochromosome carrying resistance-associated genes (Selmecki et al. 2006), and whole-ploidy shifts, aneuploidy and loss of heterozygosity constitute a general toolkit for generating phenotypic diversity quickly and, when conditions change, discarding it again (Bennett et al. 2014). In the Pacific Northwest Cryptococcus gattii outbreak lineage, copy-number variation contributes cryptic genetic variation among otherwise near-clonal isolates (Steenwyk et al. 2016).

These mechanisms are temperature-relevant in two ways. Chromosome missegregation is itself stress-sensitive, and the aneuploid states that stress generates are frequently cross-protective, conferring tolerance to challenges the population has not yet encountered (Chen et al. 2012). Aneuploidy thus sits at the boundary between the plastic and the heritable: it is inherited, but it is unstable (Tsai and Nelliat 2019).

4.4 Epimutation: Heritable without sequence change

Mucor circinelloides can become resistant to FK506 through RNA interference (RNAi), which silences the gene encoding the drug’s target without changing its DNA sequence. This silenced state, or epimutation, passes to daughter cells during cell division but can reverse when the drug is removed (Calo et al. 2014). It is a remarkable demonstration of how fungi can inherit a change in drug resistance without a DNA mutation, which would be missed by DNA sequencing. Whether heat stress can trigger a similar response remains, to our knowledge, untested, but should be a key questions for future research (Sect. 10.4).

4.5 Hsp90 as an evolutionary capacitor

The mechanistic bridge between heat tolerance and genotypic adaptation is chaperone buffering. Hsp90 stabilizes mutant proteins that would otherwise misfold, allowing standing genetic variation to accumulate in a phenotypically cryptic state; when Hsp90 capacity is exceeded, as would be expected under thermal stress, the genetic variations are uncovered and become available for selection (Rutherford et al. 2007). In fungi this was shown directly for drug resistance: Hsp90 potentiates the emergence of resistance phenotypes from pre-existing variation in C. albicans and S. cerevisiae (Cowen and Lindquist 2005). The climatic implications are obvious. A warming environment does not only generate new mutations, it also unmasks variation already present in populations. We consider this mechanism as established and its climate relevance as emerging, because the unmasking argument has not yet been demonstrated in environmental fungal populations under field-realistic thermal regimes.

4.6 Distinguishing selection from plasticity

The literature reviewed above supports the concept that thermal stress increases the generation of heritable variation in several fungal genera. However, this is not the same as a climate warming producing human pathogens by selection. The distinction rests on study design, and the requirements for the specific mechanistic of claim (Table 2).

Candida auris offers the field’s most heavily studied climate association, and the framework clarifies what that association can and cannot establish. An analysis of 801 clinical C. auris genomes from the New York–New Jersey region used Bayesian hierarchical models to relate resistance determinants and heat-tolerance genes to climatic variables. HSP90 and HSP104 were present in more than 98% of isolates, and the authors reported associations between precipitation and temperature variables and the prevalence of several resistance-associated genes and point mutations (Jara et al. 2025). The result was nevertheless, only hypothesis-generating. It is also an ecological correlation across clinical isolates whose sampling was determined by healthcare utilization, in a region of intense antifungal exposure, over a period of expanding surveillance. That design cannot separate climatic selection from the many covariates of place and time. Testing the hypothesis properly will require sampling the environment rather than the clinic, longitudinally and along a climatic gradient, with common-garden phenotyping to show that recovered isolates differ heritably in thermal tolerance and not merely in provenance.

5 Case Studies in Climate-Associated Emergence

Six emergence events follow (Fig. 4). Each is judged on its own evidence, and each subsection closes with a graded verdict on how much of the emergence climate can account for (Table 2).

5.1 Candida auris

5.1.1 A thermotolerant, halotolerant yeast without an obvious reservoir

C. auris was first described in 2009 and within a decade had been reported from every inhabited continent as a multidrug-resistant, nosocomially transmitted pathogen with a marked capacity to persist on skin and surfaces (Chowdhary et al. 2017; Forsberg et al. 2018). Its early epidemiology was genuinely anomalous: four (later five) geographically distinct clades emerged near-simultaneously on three continents, with high intra-clade and low inter-clade genetic similarity — a pattern inconsistent with a single point source spread by travel (Lockhart et al. 2017; Chow et al. 2020). European hospital outbreaks followed quickly (Schelenz et al. 2016).

The climate hypothesis proposed that C. auris was an environmental yeast of wetland or coastal habitats whose thermotolerance and halotolerance were pre-existing adaptations, and that warming allowed it to cross the thermal barrier into birds and then humans, with avian and possibly chelonian carriage explaining the intercontinental distribution (Casadevall et al. 2019). The hypothesis was notable for stating a falsifiable necessary condition: the organism should exist in the environment independent of human activity.

A necessary condition for climate-associated emergence was met when C. auris was isolated from salt marsh wetland and sandy beach habitats of the Andaman Islands, including a site with no recorded human activity, and the isolates included both multidrug-susceptible and multidrug-resistant strains clustering with clade I (Arora et al. 2021). The environmental reservoir is therefore real, and the isolate from the pristine site is difficult to explain by anthropogenic contamination.

What has not been shown is that warming caused the transition. The alternative and non-mutually-exclusive account — that C. auris was an uncommon environmental yeast that entered an unprecedented global ecological niche, the modern intensive care unit, with its dense population of immunocompromised patients, indwelling devices, azole and echinocandin exposure, and skin-to-skin and surface-mediated transmission — accounts for the epidemiology without invoking climate. The near-simultaneity of clade emergence is equally consistent with simultaneous global expansion of the niche as with simultaneous crossing of a thermal barrier. Climate-resistome correlations in clinical isolates do not resolve the question for the reasons set out in Sect. 4.6.

Hence, the environmental reservoir is now established and thermotolerance established. However, climate causation remains a hypothesis with one necessary condition satisfied. The decisive experiment of systematic environmental sampling of coastal wetlands along a latitudinal thermal gradient, with common-garden thermotolerance phenotyping, has not been done but is feasible.

5.2 Cryptococcus gattii in the Pacific Northwest

Beginning in 1999, C. gattii caused an unprecedented outbreak on Vancouver Island, eventually involving several hundred human and animal cases, with pneumonia and meningitis occurring in apparently immunocompetent hosts and the organism recoverable from native trees, soil, air and water (Kidd et al. 2004; Galanis et al. 2010). Molecular epidemiology identified a rare genotype (VGIIa) and subsequent population genomics showed that the outbreak lineages arose from a highly recombinant ancestral population, with clonal expansion following (Billmyre et al. 2014). Copy-number variation contributes additional cryptic variation within these near-clonal lineages (Steenwyk et al. 2016). The broader geography of the C. neoformans/C. gattii species complex has been interpreted in terms of continental drift and long-distance dispersal, on timescales far longer than anthropogenic warming (Kwon-Chung et al. 2014; Casadevall et al. 2017).

The hypothesis that the 1964 Great Alaska earthquake and tsunami dispersed the organism into the Pacific Northwest is attractive because it links an acute disturbance to a delayed emergence — the disaster axis operating with a multi-decade lag. However, the genomic dating and recombination structure constrain but do not establish it, and competing explanations (importation with timber or ornamental plants, unrecognized prior endemicity, climatic suitability shifts) have not been excluded.

In summary, the outbreak and environmental establishment are established facts; climatic or disaster-mediated causation is still just a hypothesis. The Cryptococcus gattii outbreak is illustrative of how a disturbance-dispersal hypothesis could be framed and tested.

5.3 Trichophyton indotineae

Formerly reported as T. mentagrophytes ITS genotype VIII and requiring sequence-based identification, T. indotineae causes extensive, inflammatory, recalcitrant dermatophytosis, is frequently terbinafine-resistant through squalene epoxidase mutations, spreads person-to-person, and has disseminated from South Asia worldwide (Uhrlaß et al. 2022). Rising terbinafine resistance has been documented in dermatophyte isolates in Denmark and France, confirming that resistance is not confined to the region of origin (Astvad et al. 2022; Moreno-Sabater et al. 2022).

The drivers for the spread of this resistant species well characterized and largely not climatic: unregulated over-the-counter combination creams containing potent topical corticosteroids, incomplete treatment courses, and crowded living conditions. Heat and humidity are established risk factors for dermatophytosis in general, and disaster settings combine skin trauma, congregate living, psychological stress and high temperature and humidity in ways that plausibly increase cutaneous fungal infection (Gupta et al. 2025). Poverty remains the dominant determinant of the global burden of superficial mycoses.

While the emergence is established and the principal drivers antifungal selection pressure and healthcare practices have been identified, the climate remains unquantified.

5.4 Coccidioides spp.

Coccidioidomycosis provides the strongest epidemiological climate signal of any human mycosis. Incidence tracks precipitation and temperature cycling, with the characteristic pattern of wet winters followed by hot dry summers favouring soil arthroconidium production and aerosolization (Head et al. 2022). Dust-storm frequency in the southwestern United States has increased and correlates with Valley fever incidence (Tong et al. 2017). Climate-envelope modelling projects substantial northward expansion of the endemic area under warming scenarios (Gorris et al. 2019), and surveillance data document rising incidence and recognition of cases outside classical endemic areas (Smith et al. 2022; Mazi et al. 2022). Occupational exposure during soil disturbance is well established: outbreaks among solar-power-farm construction workers demonstrated both the risk and the effectiveness of dust-control measures (Wilken et al. 2015; Sondermeyer Cooksey et al. 2017). The related dimorphic mycoses show comparable geographic reappraisal (Baddley 2011; Maiga et al. 2018).

Notably, this is an exposure-and-range story rather than an adaptation story: there is no evidence that Coccidioides has become more virulent or thermotolerant. Climate acts on the soil and on aerosolization, not on the pathogen’s phenotype.

Therefore, the climate association established at the level of exposure and geographic range, but no evidence of adaptation with climate change has been described . Nevertheless, coccidioidomycosis the clearest example of a climate-driven mycosis.

5.5 Sporothrix brasiliensis

The expanding zoonotic epidemic of sporotrichosis in Brazil and neighbouring countries is driven by cat-to-human transmission of S. brasiliensis, a species more virulent than S. schenckii, amplified by dense free-roaming feline populations and limited veterinary access (Rodrigues et al. 2013; Gremião et al. 2017; Rossow et al. 2020). Urbanization, poverty and animal-population dynamics are the main factors that drive epidemics. While temperature and humidity plausibly modulate environmental persistence, these climate factors have not been shown to drive expansion.

5.6 Aspergillus fumigatus

The clearest example of human-driven adaptive evolution in a fungal pathogen involves no clear climate component. Environmental use of demethylase-inhibitor fungicides that share a target with medical triazoles selects azole-resistant A. fumigatus in agricultural and waste-composting substrates (Snelders et al. 2012). The resistance mechanisms include specific cyp51A alterations with characteristic tandem-repeat promoter insertions (Verweij et al. 2009; Snelders et al. 2012; Berger et al. 2017), a development that raised the question of whether mould-active azoles can be retained for clinical use at all (Verweij et al. 2015). Population genomics has since confirmed that patients acquire resistant genotypes from the environment rather than generating them during therapy (Rhodes et al. 2022), and the wider threat of antifungal resistance to human health is now recognized as a policy problem (Fisher et al. 2018, 2022). The evolution of resistance in environmental compartments has become a field in its own right (van Rhijn and Rhodes 2025), supported by curated resistance-mutation resources (Bédard et al. 2025).

This case is the right comparator for weighing climate claims for three reasons. The exposure is quantifiable, the selective agent is identified, and the causal chain from environmental compartment to patient has been closed with genomic evidence. No climate–fungus association currently meets that standard. It also illustrates that anthropogenic chemical pressure, unlike temperature, acts on a specific molecular target, which is why its evolutionary signature was detectable.

Human-driven adaptive evolution of Aspergillus fumigatus is established, with a closed causal chain arising from agricultural fungicides. Climate may interact with this system, through fungicide persistence, crop pathogen pressure and compost temperature, but the demonstrated driver is chemical, not thermal.

6 Anthropogenic Co-drivers Beyond Temperature

A common misconception is to attribute to “climate change” effects that are produced by other components of global environmental change. Some examples of these anthropogenic co-drivers are described below.

6.1 Agricultural and industrial antifungal inputs

Fungicide application is the largest deliberate chemical selection pressure humans impose on fungi. Crop pests and pathogens have been shown to shift poleward at measurable rates as temperature isotherms move (Bebber et al. 2013), which means fungicide use and warming are coupled: warming changes where fungicides are applied, and fungicide application changes the resistance landscape the environmental mycobiota experiences. Disentangling the two requires explicit modelling of both (van Rhijn and Rhodes 2025). An analogous coupling has been demonstrated for bacterial antimicrobial resistance, where local temperature is associated with higher resistance prevalence independent of prescribing (MacFadden et al. 2018), and climate–resistance interactions are now being treated as a distinct research agenda (Kalanxhi and Laxminarayan 2026).

6.2 Plastic pollution and plastiphily

Plastic waste creates a novel, globally distributed terrestrial substrate. Microplastics in soil accumulate a distinct mycobiota enriched in human-pathogenic genera, indicating that the plastisphere functions as a selective niche rather than an inert surface (Gkoutselis et al. 2021). Subsequent work linked plastiphily, the capacity to colonize and metabolize plastic, to generic virulence-associated traits in important human pathogenic fungi (Gkoutselis et al. 2024). The mechanistic question remains: does growth on microplastic substrates alter growth rate, morphogenesis, oxidative-stress tolerance, antifungal susceptibility, interaction with phagocytes, or virulence in infection models? A factorial design comparing standard agar with agar supplemented with defined microplastic particles, read out across those six phenotypes, could answer this question directly (Sect. 10.1).

6.3 Urbanization and urban heat islands

Cities warm faster than their surroundings and therefore constitute accessible natural experiments in thermal selection, with the paired-neighbourhood experimenal design of Smith et al Smith et al. (2025) serving as the model (Sect. 3.2). Urbanization simultaneously changes host density, healthcare access, animal reservoirs and travel connectivity, all of which shape infectious disease independently of temperature (Baker et al. 2022). The urban heat island is therefore both the best available field laboratory for thermal adaptation studies but also and one of the most confounded.

6.4 Ultraviolet radiation, carbon dioxide, aridity and pollutants

Temperature is only one of dozens of environmental conditions changing at the same time. The standard lists of variables that satellites track for climate monitoring run to dozens of measurements of the air, ocean and land, and many of them matter for fungi: how wet the soil is, how hot the ground surface gets, where fires burn, where permafrost thaws, how land is covered and how much plant material it holds. A study that tests fungal outcomes against only one or two of these, ignoring the rest, may credit temperature with changes that really come from drying, sunlight or the way people use the land. Pigment and radiation biology do offer a believable mechanism for ultraviolet light specifically driving selection (Dadachova et al. 2007; Cordero and Casadevall 2017), but no one has tested that idea as a climate question.

6.5 Travel, trade and migration

How connected the world is decides how fast a strain that adapts in one place becomes everyone’s problem. Researchers have already looked at how travel volume, city growth and climate act together for infectious disease in general (Baker et al. 2022), and climate change is expected to give pathogens more chances to jump between species as animals shift where they live (Carlson et al. 2022); disease and planetary change are now treated as one field of study (Carlson et al. 2025). One review of the published evidence found that most diseases known to infect people can be made worse by climate hazards, and by several different routes (Mora et al. 2022). For fungi, the spread argument carries the most weight for strains that resist antifungal drugs and for organisms passed on inside hospitals, where the major mode of transport and dispersal is patients and staff, not the wind.

7 Disaster Microbiology: Acute Perturbations

7.1 Defining the field

7.2 Wind: Tornadoes and tropical cyclones

The most illustrative case series comes from the is the 2011 Joplin, Missouri F5 tornado, after which a cluster of necrotizing cutaneous mucormycosis due to Apophysomyces species occurred among injured survivors, with penetrating trauma and wound contamination as the shared exposure and high mortality despite surgery and antifungal therapy (Neblett Fanfair et al. 2012). Two features of these infections have long been puzzling: their aggressiveness in previously healthy people, and their occurrence in organisms that are ubiquitous yet rarely cause such disease. The shear-stress experiments discussed in Sect. 3.3 offer a mechanistic hypothesis — that the disaster event itself transiently reprogrammes the fungus toward hypervirulence before inoculation (Wurster et al. 2020). This experimental evidence also points to a clear prediction: isolates recovered early after such events should show the transient phenotype, which should decay in culture. To our knowledge this prediction has not been tested on outbreak isolates.

7.3 Water: Floods, humidity and sewage

Flooding produces the most consistent fungal exposure of any disaster type. After Hurricane Katrina, airborne and settled mould concentrations in flooded New Orleans homes far exceeded those in non-flooded reference homes, and endotoxin and glucan burdens were high; Aspergillus and Penicillium were prominent among the recovered taxa (Chew et al. 2006; Rao et al. 2007). Invasive disease, however, remains the exception. It concentrates in immunocompromised residents and in those with heavy remediation exposure, and surveillance after Hurricane Harvey documented few invasive infections in the affected population (Toda et al. 2023). Floodwater mixed with sewage also disperses organisms and resistance determinants across the built environment. This mechanism is better characterized for bacteria than for fungi and warrants further studies in fungi.

7.4 Fire: Pyroaerobiology

Wildfire smoke was long treated as a chemical and particulate hazard. It is also a biological one: smoke plumes carry viable microbial cells, including fungi, and wildland fire alters the composition and diversity of the atmospheric microbiome, with smoke acting as a transport medium rather than a sterilizing agent (Kobziar et al. 2022). Dispersal of microbes from smoke into soils downwind has since been demonstrated (Ellington et al. 2024). The clinical corollary, that wildfire could expand the range or exposure burden of soil-dwelling dimorphic fungi such as Coccidioides, is a plausible hypothesis with epidemiological support from soil-disturbance occupational outbreaks (Wilken et al. 2015; Sondermeyer Cooksey et al. 2017), but direct evidence that fire-borne propagules cause human infection has yet to be established. The field is young and, given the growing frequency of wildfires, will become a more important filed of investigation.

7.5 Earth and ice: Seismic events and permafrost

Earthquakes and tsunamis cause fungal disease through aspiration of contaminated water and direct wound inoculation; case series after natural disasters document pulmonary and cutaneous mycoses, including mucormycosis and aspergillosis (Benedict and Park 2014). Permafrost thaw has attracted attention as a potential reservoir of long-dormant organisms, but the evidence consists of laboratory work in which viable microorganisms and giant viruses were recovered and revived from permafrost cores tens of thousands of years old (Wu et al. 2022; Alempic et al. 2023). The fact that such organisms can survive and resume replication after prolonged freezing is a demonstration of physical possibility in fungi. No human infection has been traced to a thaw-released organism, and their is no evidence that revived organisms are infectious to humans, how they would reach a susceptible host, or at what rate thaw releases them. For fungi in particular, the more plausible risk is ecological: cold-adapted taxa expanding into warming soils, rather than the emergence of an ancient human pathogen.

7.6 The host side of disaster mycology

Disasters act at least as strongly on hosts as on fungi. Barrier disruption through trauma, penetrating woulds and burns, congregate sheltering, interruption of antifungal prophylaxis and immunosuppressive regimens, displacement, malnutrition and psychological stress all increase susceptibility. Climate exposures themselves have measurable effects on immune function, with evidence spanning innate and adaptive compartments, epithelial barrier integrity, oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory signalling (Imberti et al. 2025). Warming and pollution are independently implicated in epithelial barrier dysfunction in the airway and skin (Çelebi Sözener et al. 2023). Insect bites, which shift with warming-driven changes in arthropod distribution, are an underappreciated route of cutaneous inoculation of moulds in immunocompromised patients (Kontoyiannis et al. 2019), and the possibility that warming increases invertebrate-associated cutaneous invasive fungal infection (Kontoyiannis and Casadevall 2025). These host-side effects fall disproportionately on poor populations, in whom pre-existing immune compromise, where occupational exposure s and delayed care are often compounded (Jenks et al. 2023).

8 Ecosystem and One Health Dimensions

8.1 Plant pathogens, food security and mycotoxins

The climate signal in plant-pathogenic fungi is better established than any comparable signal in medical mycology. Crop pests and pathogens have shifted poleward at rates consistent with the movement of warming isotherms (Bebber et al. 2013), and infection risk for major crop pathogens tracks climate variables that also govern yield (Chaloner et al. 2021). Together these provide a measured direction and rate of range change, tied to a climate variable, across many pathogen species.

The consequences for food security and the adaptation options available have been synthesized in detail (Singh et al. 2023). Mycotoxin risk is shifting alongside this range expansion. Modelling of aflatoxin B1 in European maize projects higher contamination risk under warming scenarios, with the northern limit of significant risk moving into regions that are currently unaffected (Battilani et al. 2016). Reviews of climate effects on mycotoxin-producing fungi and their toxin profiles reach concordant conclusions (Medina et al. 2017; Zingales et al. 2022; Casu et al. 2024). These patterns are of interest because mycotoxin production is a stress-responsive trait in fungi (Schmidt-Heydt et al. 2008). The same warming that moves these fungi also changes what they synthesize once they arrive.

8.2 Wildlife panzootics as natural thermal experiments

The clearest demonstrations that climate-associated fungal disease can cause mass mortality come from ectothermic and heterothermic animals, not humans. Chytridiomycosis caused by Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis has driven the greatest documented loss of biodiversity attributable to a single pathogen, affecting hundreds of amphibian species (Scheele et al. 2019; Fisher and Garner 2020). Its congener B. salamandrivorans emerged subsequently as a lethal pathogen of salamanders (Martel et al. 2013). Thermal biology is central: B. dendrobatidis grows optimally well below mammalian body temperature and its thermal performance varies among isolates (Stevenson et al. 2013), and climate and temperature variability have been linked to the pattern of amphibian declines (Rohr and Raffel 2010). White-nose syndrome, caused by the cold-adapted Pseudogymnoascus destructans, kills hibernating bats precisely because torpor lowers body temperature into the fungus’s permissive range (Blehert et al. 2009).

These systems support the central mechanistic claim that disease occurs where host and fungal thermal ranges overlap. The mammalian thermal barrier is a quantitative constraint: raising host temperature above the pathogen’s ceiling is protective, while lowering it is potentially catastrophic. They also show where the consequences of warming have landed to date. The documented harms are ecological, falling on amphibians, other ectotherms and plants, rather than clinical in humans. No comparable warming-attributable burden of human fungal disease has yet been demonstrated (Fisher et al. 2012; Case et al. 2025).

8.3 Fungi as mitigators of human climatic effects

A chapter that treats fungi only as hazards would misrepresent the kingdom. Fungal catabolic versatility underpins bioremediation of hydrocarbons, pesticides, dyes and other hazardous chemicals (Harms et al. 2011), and fungal degradation of recalcitrant synthetic polymers, including polyester–polyurethane by Aspergillus tubingensis, is an active applied field (Khan et al. 2017). At ecosystem scale, mycorrhizal networks constitute one of the largest annual terrestrial carbon fluxes (Hawkins et al. 2023), and fungal contributions to Earth-system processes are now argued to warrant explicit representation in global change science (Case et al. 2025). How fungi respond to a warming planet therefore matters well beyond infectious disease. The same physiological and distributional shifts that determine pathogenic potential also govern the degradative and symbiotic functions, including the mycorrhizal carbon flux.

9 Is a Human Fungal Pandemic Plausible?

The popular imagination has been captured by the premise of a thermally adapted fungus turning epidemic in humans — a premise drawn from entomopathogenic fungi that manipulate insect behaviour (Hughes et al. 2011) and elaborated in video games and television series such as The LAst of Us. The scientific version of the argument is more measured: warming increases the number of thermotolerant fungal species, urban warming accelerates fungal growth, the thermal gradient between mammals and environmental fungi is narrowing from both ends, and the overwhelming majority of fungal species remain uncharacterized (Garcia-Solache and Casadevall 2010; Casadevall 2020; Nnadi and Carter 2021). The arguments against a human fungal pandemic are that the thermal exclusion gradient remains wide (Sect. 2.2); thermal adaptation in natural populations is slow relative to the timescale of human existance, and the assembly of pathogenicity requires multiple traits beyond thermotolerance alone (Rokas 2022). Moreover, relative to other pathogens (e.g, bacterial and viral) fungal host tropism is typically narrow, with no precedent for a host jump of the required magnitude.

A fungal lineage capable of pandemic behaviour in humans would need to demonstrate: (i) heritable growth and replication at 36–37 °C, not merely survival; (ii) evasion of innate immune effectors in immunocompetent hosts, not only in neutropenic or transplant populations; (iii) an efficient route of human-to-human transmission, or an environmental exposure route of very high density; (iv) a reservoir in which selection is sustained between outbreaks; and (v) sufficient genetic variability to escape the small number of available antifungal classes.

Assessed against these criteria, C. auris is perhaps the most instructive: it satisfies (i), (iv) and (v), partially satisfies (iii) through healthcare-associated transmission, and fails (ii), since disease remains concentrated in patients with substantial comorbidity. Crucially, C. auris did not need to acquire thermotolerance — it appears already to have possessed it (Casadevall et al. 2019; Arora et al. 2021). The organism that comes closest to pandemic criteria therefore does so by a route that seems to have little to do with global warming.

These criteria translate into indicators that existing surveillance could track. Five are worth specifying: (i) recovery of thermotolerant opportunists from environments where they have not previously been detected; (ii) emergence of resistance within a single host during therapy, in genera not previously known for it; (iii) clusters of invasive disease in immunocompetent people; (iv) antifungal resistance appearing in environmental isolates before it appears in clinical ones (van Rhijn and Rhodes 2025; Bédard et al. 2025); and (v) post-disaster infection clusters involving unusual organisms. None of these depends on new technology. Each depends on sustained sampling, consistent species-level identification, and linkage between environmental and clinical datasets.

10 A Research and Response Agenda

10.1 Experimental systems

Progress in this field is limited by model systems more than by ideas. Invertebrate mini-host models, in particular Drosophila melanogaster, allow throughput-scale virulence comparison across many isolates and temperatures and have been validated for the study of medically important fungi, including Mucorales (Chamilos et al. 2006, 2008). Their central advantage for climate work is poikilothermy: host temperature becomes an independent experimental variable rather than a fixed constant, which no mammalian model permits. A design of this kind — several T. indotineae isolates grown at 37 °C and 42 °C, with and without topical corticosteroid exposure, compared against T. rubrum and Rhizopus controls in a thoracic-pricking fly model — would test strain-to-strain variability in thermal virulence enhancement in a dermatophyte, which to our knowledge has not been done.

For cutaneous pathogens, three-dimensional human tissue systems can serve as a complementary model. Reconstructed human epidermis, skin organoids and porcine or human skin explants permit infection at physiological skin surface temperature, roughly 32–34 °C, rather than core temperature, which is the thermal niche that matters for dermatophytes.

Adaptation itself calls for a different design. Serial passage or continuous culture under controlled thermal regimes remains the decisive approach, because it is the only one that distinguishes heritable change from reversible acclimation. As proof of principle, continuous culture of Metarhizium anisopliae over 22 growth-and-dilution cycles produced variants growing robustly at 36.5–37 °C, a temperature inhibitory to the parent strain, while retaining insect pathogenicity (de Crécy et al. 2009). The advatnages of such an experimental system are worth highlighting. The founder population is archived at the outset, and evolved and ancestral lines are re-phenotyped side by side under identical conditions at the end, so that any difference between them reflects evolved change rather than the conditions under which each was grown (Table 2).

10.2 Integrating climate data with genomic surveillance

The gap identified in Sect. 4.6 can be closed. What it calls for is sampling the environment rather than patients, along gradients where climate differs in a known way: from south to north, from city centre to surrounding countryside, or at one site before and after a disturbance such as a flood. Every isolate would be stored, sequenced, then grown and tested in the laboratory alongside the others under identical conditions, so that any difference between them reflects the organisms themselves rather than the conditions they came from. Competing explanations would be measured rather than assumed-i.e. exposure to antifungal drugs and agricultural fungicides, how the land is used, how many hosts are present, and how hard anyone has been looking. Standard climate datasets already provide matching temperature and moisture records at a fine enough scale. The same design works at any size. Its smallest form compares paired city neighbourhoods that differ in summer temperature (Smith et al. 2025). Its largest would sample the environment across several countries for Candidozyma auris, testing the wetland origin hypothesis directly.

10.3 Multidisciplinary and policy infrastructure

The questions raised here cannot be answered by mycology researchers alone. They require collaboration with climatology experts and remote sensing for exposure modelling, engineering and building science for post-disaster remediation, veterinary and wildlife science for reservoir work, social science and health economics for the susceptibility gradient, and public health policy for surveillance mandates. Fungal disease remains largely absent from national climate–health adaptation planning despite the WHO priority pathogen framework (Fisher and Denning 2023), and antifungal resistance sits awkwardly outside much of the antimicrobial-resistance policy architecture (Fisher et al. 2022; Kalanxhi and Laxminarayan 2026). Clearly, invasive fungal diseases should be included in climate–health surveillance, and environmental antifungal-resistance monitoring alongside agricultural fungicide reporting. Public and professional education is the third, and the least expensive policy goal for future programs.

10.4 Ten Priority Questions

  1. How do interactions with plants, bacteria and amoebae in the environment shape fungal virulence and resistance traits that later manifest in mammalian hosts?
  2. What is the eco-evolutionary framework for fungal thermal adaptation, and which variables besides mean temperature — precipitation, diurnal and seasonal thermal variance, light regime, pollution, urbanization — modulate it?
  3. Is climate-associated adaptation in fungal pathogens driven by genetic microevolution and selection, by reversible plasticity, or by epigenetic mechanisms such as epimutation?
  4. Can thermal stress, as distinct from drug exposure, induce epimutation?
  5. Does growth on microplastic substrates alter fungal growth, morphogenesis, stress tolerance, antifungal susceptibility, phagocyte interaction and virulence?
  6. Do post-disaster clinical isolates display the transient hypervirulent phenotype predicted by shear-stress experiments, and how quickly does it decay in culture?
  7. Which fungal pathogens are climate-insensitive, and what does their insensitivity teach us about the mechanisms operating in the sensitive ones?
  8. Does wildfire smoke transport viable propagules of dimorphic pathogens at epidemiologically relevant doses?
  9. What is the relative contribution of climatic versus antifungal-selection pressure to the emergence of resistant dermatophytes and A. fumigatus genotypes?
  10. Can environmental surveillance detect thermotolerant opportunists before they appear clinically, and at what cost per detection?

11 Conclusions

Three conclusions can be summarized from the currently available evidence on the effects of climate change on fungi. First, the mechanistic case that thermal and physical stress change fungal phenotype and genotype is now strong and no longer speculative: growth at elevated temperature increases virulence through defined signalling pathways, host temperature mobilizes transposable elements and induces mutations that confer resistance and hypervirulence, and physical stresses characteristic of extreme weather act through the same stress-integration architecture. These are laboratory-established mechanisms with identified molecular components, not correlations.

Second, the epidemiological case that climate change has produced specific new human fungal pathogens remains unproven, and the gap is one of study design rather than of plausibility. The emergence most often cited as evidence — C. auris, T. indotineae, azole-resistant A. fumigatus — each have better-documented non-climatic drivers, while the mycosis with the clearest climate signal, coccidioidomycosis, involves no fungal adaptation at all. Where climate-associated fungal disease has demonstrably caused mass mortality, it has been in ectothermic and hibernating animals, exactly where thermal overlap between host and pathogen is greatest.

Third, the prospect of a human fungal pandemic driven by thermal adaptation remains improbable based on current evidence, and stating this clearly should be a scientific responsibility of the mycology field. The credible risks are more mundane and tractable: wider geographic exposure to established pathogens, more frequent post-disaster infection clusters, an enlarging environmental reservoir of antifungal resistance, and a growing population of susceptible hosts. All four are addressable with existing tools, and none requires a pandemic to justify attention.
